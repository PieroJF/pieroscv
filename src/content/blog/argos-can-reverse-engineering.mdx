During my time as an Automation Engineer at Argos, an oil and gas automation company in
Mexico, I ran into a wall: a motor driver that would only talk to its vendor's software.
The way through it was the approach that ended up defining that job for me: find the
seam, script it.

## The US$25,000 driver nobody could talk to

The team was working to bring microcontroller-based control into high-temperature,
high-pressure downhole environments. For that purpose the company had purchased a motor
driver rated for those conditions, valued at US$25,000. The problem was that driving it
required either an even more expensive dedicated microcontroller, or a PC running the
vendor's proprietary software with an Arduino acting purely as a bridge to reach the
driver.

In theory a third-party microcontroller could control the driver. In practice, the
vendor's support ended at sharing the meaning of each hexadecimal byte transmitted over
the CAN bus. They never disclosed the actual communication protocol or the real control
sequence the driver expected.

## A man-in-the-middle made of Arduino

I was assigned the task of finding a way to control that motor driver directly from an
Arduino over CAN bus. My approach was hands on: I set up a second Arduino as a
man-in-the-middle between the PC and the driver, running a C script that intercepted the
raw byte strings the proprietary software was sending and logged every frame to a text
file.

With the captured traffic on one side and the vendor's hexadecimal byte definitions on
the other, I reverse-engineered the driver's communication and control sequences and
reproduced them in my own code. The result: a US$25,000 motor driver controlled from a
standard Arduino over CAN bus, no proprietary software and no dedicated controller
required.

What I like most about this one is that it needed no exotic tooling. A spare Arduino, a
C script, a log file and patience were enough to open up hardware that was effectively
locked to one vendor's stack.

## Takeaway

A locked-down driver looks like a dead end, but somewhere underneath there is always a
bus you can sniff. The devil is in the details, and the details are usually documented,
one hexadecimal byte at a time.
