For our Applied Robotics module at the University of Birmingham, a team of four of us
built an autonomous fruit-sorting system: a Quanser QArm 4-DOF manipulator with an Intel
RealSense D415 RGB-D camera, sorting 14 fruits into 3 baskets. I've already written about
[the inverse kinematics](/blog/qarm-fruit-sorting). This post covers the part of the
project I owned: camera–arm calibration — specifically the day it failed in the lab, and
the roughly fifteen commits it took me, one day later, to rebuild trust in it.

## The pick that missed

On 21 April 2026 we ran the full autonomous pipeline on hardware for the first time. The
robot scanned the table, classified a fruit, planned the approach, descended, closed the
gripper — on air. The post-mortem number was brutally clear: a **44.8 mm calibration
residual** between where the camera said the fruit was and where the arm actually went.
Our gripper forgives roughly 10 mm. Everything downstream — inverse kinematics accurate
to 0.0002 mm, spline trajectories, the state machine — had executed flawlessly on
coordinates that were simply wrong.

That's what makes calibration bugs nasty: nothing crashes. Every module does correct
arithmetic in a frame that doesn't match reality.

## Deleting our own work

Our original calibration was a closed-loop rig with a secondary UGreen webcam watching
the arm — work I had put real hours into, including diagnosing a hang in `cap.set()` and
an image-diff tracker whose TCP detection broke whenever someone walked through the
background of the lab. On 22 April the team made the right call: throw it away. Two days
of work, deleted on purpose, replaced by a single-camera pipeline built around a printed
chessboard lying on the work surface. The redesign and the detector rewrite that day were
largely my teammates' push, and the payoff was immediate: per-session calibration setup
dropped from **~20 minutes to ~30 seconds**.

Sunk cost argues loudly in the middle of a two-week sprint. It was still the correct
decision — and it set up the day this post is really about.

## Fifteen commits on 24 April

A chessboard makes calibration fast; it does not make it correct. My job on 24 April was
making the board → camera → robot chain trustworthy, and the day resolved into four
fixes, visible commit by commit.

**1. Corner-order ambiguity.** OpenCV's `findChessboardCorners` returns the grid of
interior corners, but the ordering it picks depends on how the board sits in the image.
Same board, slightly different viewpoint, and the corners get enumerated from a different
edge — which silently builds a coordinate frame rotated relative to the previous
session's. My fix: sweep all **four candidate orientations** of the corner grid, score
each against the expected geometry, and construct the board frame from **three corners**
— an origin plus two edge directions — orthogonalized with **Gram–Schmidt**, so pixel
noise can't smuggle a shear into what must be a pure rotation.

**2. The right solver for a plane.** A chessboard is coplanar, the degenerate case that
general-purpose PnP solvers handle worst. I switched the extrinsics solve to
**`SOLVEPNP_IPPE`**, OpenCV's solver built specifically for planar targets.

**3. Rejecting the mirror world.** Planar PnP has a known two-fold ambiguity: a single
image admits two consistent poses, one of them mirror-inverted through the board plane.
Occasionally the solver preferred the impossible one — a camera below the table. I added
a guard that rejects any extrinsic placing the camera on the wrong side of the plane. Not
unlikely: unrepresentable.

**4. Capturing while still moving.** Some calibration frames were being grabbed while the
arm was still settling into its survey pose. I tightened the capture settle tolerance
from **0.05 to 0.015 rad**, so a frame is only taken once the joints have genuinely
stopped.

## What it bought us

After that day, calibration stopped being a topic. Thirty seconds at the start of a lab
session, a residual check, and on to the actual sorting.

The lesson I keep from it: calibration errors never announce themselves, so the pipeline
has to reject wrong answers structurally — orientation sweeps instead of trusting an
ordering, a guard instead of hoping the solver picks the physical solution, a settle gate
instead of assuming stillness. Trust in a robot's perception isn't something you verify
once; it's something you enforce every session, in thirty seconds.
